Concept 1

What a comparator does

Key words

Comparator
compares two voltages and switches its output.
Op-amp
the chip used as a comparator. Ours is the 741.
+ input
pin 3. Also called the non-inverting input.
− input
pin 2. Also called the inverting input.

A comparator has two inputs. It looks at which input has the bigger voltage.

  • The + input is bigger: the output is high, close to +9 V.
  • The − input is bigger: the output is low, close to 0 V.

The output has only these two states. It switches from one to the other. It is a digital output.

Pin 7 goes to +9 V and pin 4 to 0 V. These power the chip.

+ bigger: output high.

− bigger: output low.

Practice 1

A comparator has these input voltages:

  • + input = 4.5 V
  • − input = 5.0 V

State whether the output is high or low. Give a reason.

Answer
Low. The − input is bigger than the + input.

  • name the + input, the − input and the output of a 741.
  • say when the output is high and when it is low.
Concept 2

Two voltage dividers

Key words

Voltage divider
two resistors that share the supply voltage.
Thermistor
its resistance falls as it warms up.
Vin
the input voltage. It changes with temperature.
Vref
the reference voltage. It stays the same. Exam papers may call it Vx.

Each input of the comparator comes from a voltage divider.

  • TH1 over VR1 makes Vin. It goes to the + input.
  • R2 over R3 makes Vref. It goes to the − input.

In a divider, the bigger resistor gets the bigger share of the supply.

When TH1 warms up, its resistance falls. VR1 now gets a bigger share. So Vin rises.

R2 and R3 are fixed. So Vref stays the same.

Voltage across the bottom resistor
V2 = R2R1 + R2 × Vs
R1 = top resistor (Ω) · R2 = bottom resistor (Ω) · Vs = supply voltage (V) · V2 = voltage across the bottom resistor (V)
Worked example A reference divider has 10 kΩ on top and 5.0 kΩ below. The supply is 12 V. Calculate Vref.
Vref=R2R1 + R2 × Vs
Vref=(5.0 ÷ (10 + 5.0)) × 12both in kΩ, so no need to convert
Vref=4.0 V
Practice 2

An input divider is on a 9.0 V supply:

  • TH1 (top) = 6.0 kΩ
  • VR1 (bottom) = 3.0 kΩ

(a) Calculate Vin.

(b) State whether the output is high or low when Vref = 1.6 V.

Answer

(a) Vin = (3.0 ÷ (6.0 + 3.0)) × 9.0 = 3.0 V

(b) High. Vin (3.0 V) is bigger than Vref (1.6 V).

You can now…

  • calculate the voltage from a divider.
  • say why Vin rises as TH1 warms up.

Concept 4

How the circuit works

Key words

Variable resistor
a resistor you can adjust. VR1 sets the temperature at which LED1 comes on.
Describe
give the steps in order. Each step is worth one mark.

To describe how it works, follow the chain from the sensor to the output:

  • As the temperature rises, the resistance of TH1 falls.
  • So Vin rises.
  • When Vin is bigger than Vref, the output goes high and LED1 comes on.

VR1 sets the temperature at which this happens. Turn VR1 up: Vin is bigger at every temperature, so LED1 comes on when it is colder.

Another way to build it

Swap TH1 for a fixed resistor, R1. Swap VR1 for an LDR. Now LED1 comes on when it gets dark, like a night light.

The rest of the circuit stays the same.

Practice 4

Describe how the night-light version switches LED1 on as it gets dark. (3)

Answer
  • As it gets darker, the resistance of the LDR rises (1).
  • So Vin rises (1).
  • When Vin is bigger than Vref, the output goes high and LED1 comes on (1).

Concept 5

Test it in Yenka

Key words

Voltmeter
measures the voltage at a point. Connect it from the point to 0 V.
Logic indicator
shows the logic level. In Yenka, red = high and white = low.

In Yenka you can't touch a test point with a probe. Add meters to the circuit instead.

  • A voltmeter on Vin (where T3 is).
  • A voltmeter on Vref (where T4 is).
  • A logic indicator on the output (where T5 is).

Change the temperature of TH1. Vref should not move. Vin should rise as it warms. The logic indicator turns red when Vin passes Vref.

Practice 5

In Yenka, the meters read:

  • Vin = 1.2 V
  • Vref = 1.6 V

State the colour of the logic indicator, and whether LED1 is on.

Answer
White, and LED1 is off. Vin is smaller than Vref, so the output is low.

You can now…

  • add voltmeters and a logic indicator to a Yenka circuit.
  • read a logic indicator: red is high, white is low.
Concept 6

Comparator or transistor switch?

Key words

Transistor switch
a divider feeds the base of a transistor. It turns on when the base reaches about 0.7 V.
Switching point
the temperature (or light level) at which the output comes on.

A transistor switch can do the same job. The same divider, TH1 over VR1, feeds the base of transistor TR1 through a base resistor.

As TH1 warms up, the base voltage rises. At about 0.7 V, TR1 turns on and LED1 lights.

So the transistor always switches at about 0.7 V. The comparator switches at Vref, a voltage you choose.

See Transistor switching circuits for more.

Transistor switch

Pros

  • Fewer parts, so it is cheaper and simpler to build.
  • It can switch a bigger current, such as a relay.

Cons

  • It turns on gradually. LED1 can glow dimly near the switching point.
  • It always switches at about 0.7 V on the base.
  • The switching point drifts as the transistor itself warms up.

Comparator

Pros

  • It switches sharply: the output is fully high or fully low.
  • It switches at Vref, any voltage you choose.
  • The switching point is precise and easy to set with VR1.

Cons

  • More parts: an IC and a reference divider. It costs more.
  • Its output current is small. It can light an LED, but a relay or motor needs a transistor after it.

Many circuits use both. The comparator decides when to switch. A transistor after it drives the big load.

Practice 6

A greenhouse heater must switch on at exactly 12 °C.

State whether a transistor switch or a comparator is better. Give a reason.

Answer
A comparator. It switches sharply at a set point, and VR1 lets you set that point precisely. (The heater itself would need a transistor or relay after the comparator.)
