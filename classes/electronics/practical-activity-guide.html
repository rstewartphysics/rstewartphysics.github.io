This page runs alongside your mock assignment booklet — it does not replace it. What you do at each stage is in the booklet; what is here is one hint per stage, how marks get lost there, and a link to the right tool. Hints, never answers — the design is yours to work out.

This is a mock. It is marked exactly like the real practical activity, but the marks do not count towards your qualification — they show you and your teacher what to work on first. In the real assessment you would not have this page.
Before you ask your teacher, work down this list. Most of the time you will not reach the bottom.
  1. Read the stage again in your booklet, and re-read the advice pages at the front.
  2. Look back at your own earlier work. Your block diagram, truth table, simulation printout and layouts answer most questions about what comes next.
  3. You are on the class website now — scroll down for a hint on the stage you are stuck on, and the tool that goes with it.
  4. Use the tool. The Block Diagram Builder, the Stripboard Builder and the simulation, construction and testing pages are all on the site.
  5. Only then ask your teacher — and say what you have already tried. Working independently is worth 6 marks across construction and wiring.
The brief

Pillar drill warning system

A pillar drill at Berwickshire Engineering is in use all day. One warning unit must warn the supervisor if the motor is overheating, or the emergency stop has been pressed, or the guard has been left open. The workshop is noisy and ear defenders are worn, so the warning has to be seen as well as heard. The real sensors are not available in the lab, so each one is simulated by a switch.

Your three inputs

Each one is an SPDT (changeover) switch with a 10 kΩ pull-down resistor.

InputSimulatesLogic 1 means
AMotor temperature sensorthe motor is too hot
BEmergency stopthe emergency stop has been pressed
CGuard interlockthe guard is closed
Read this one twice. Input C reads logic 1 when the machine is safe, not when there is a fault. Think carefully about what that means for your design.

The full brief and the specification the process and output must meet are on booklet p.3–4 — read those pages properly, don't work from this screen alone.

Overview

The ten stages

Tap a number to jump to that stage. Stages 1 to 6 design the system and plan the build on paper. Stages 7, 8 and 9 build one sub-system at a time — each board is finished and working before the next one is started — and your assessor checks and signs every stage before you begin the next.

The ten stages are the order you work in; the five tasks are what your assessor records the marks against — see how the practical activity is marked. Keep your record of progress up to date at every stage (booklet p.7–8).

Stages 1 – 3

Designing and costing

Read the brief. Decide what each switch means. Draw the system, prove it on screen, then price it.

Stage 1 — Block diagram 4 marks

Task 1a · Booklet p.9–10

For each input, write down what logic 1 means. Then ask: does the warning need to come on when this input is 1, or when it is 0? An input that is the wrong way round has to be turned round inside your system.

How marks get lost here
  • Elements listed rather than joined up with arrows.
  • The process section left vague.
  • Sections not ruled apart.
Block Diagram Builder — twelve systems to practise on → Logic gates and truth tables (Theory) →
Stage 2 — Simulation in Yenka 3 marks

Task 2a · Booklet p.11

Build it a gate at a time and test after each one. A simulation that was working two gates ago is much easier to fix.

How marks get lost here
  • Only the fault states demonstrated, not all eight.
  • Printout attached with nothing marked on it.
Using simulation software — why, when and how →
Stage 3 — Costing 100 prototypes 3 marks

Task 1b · Booklet p.12

Look the parts up on Rapid or RS. A batch of 100 resistors usually costs less than ten bought singly — that is why the marks are for batch pricing.

How marks get lost here
  • Item IDs never written back onto the Stage 2 simulation printout.
  • Unit price times quantity, instead of pricing by the batch.
  • Batches not rounded up to a whole batch.
  • DIL sockets, the stripboard itself or the wire left off the list.

Stages 4 – 6

Planning the build

The boards, the wiring and the testing are all planned on paper before a single track is cut.

Stage 4 — Stripboard schematic diagrams 4 marks

Task 2b · Booklet p.13

Count the holes you need before you draw anything, and leave a spare row at each end. Mark the track cuts and the test points first, then fit the components round them.

How marks get lost here
  • Track cuts missing.
  • Test points not planned onto the layout — fit them here, not when you are already soldering.
  • Parts drawn but not labelled with their item ID.
  • Sheets not attached to the booklet — they cannot be marked.
Stripboard Builder — lay out a board and mark the cuts → Construction — boards, track cuts, resistor codes →
Stage 5 — Wiring schedule marked with 7–9

Task 3b · Booklet p.13

Decide your colours before you cut any wire, and write them into the schedule. Re-doing a loom costs a lesson.

How marks get lost here
  • Red or black used for a signal.
  • One colour used for every signal — that says every one of those points carries the same signal.
  • Wires made but never written into the schedule.
Construction — cables, colour coding and looming →
Stage 6 — Test planning 3 marks

Task 4a · Booklet p.14–18

Write each description as two halves: what you will do, and what you expect to see. If you cannot say what you expect, you do not yet know what the test is for.

How marks get lost here
  • Descriptions written after the testing instead of before it.
  • Tables left blank until the board is built.
  • Every reading written as a voltage — use a logic probe at the gate pins and record the logic level.
  • The ninth table's simulation column left empty.
Testing — meters, methods and checklists → Testing videos (below) →
Stages 7 – 9

One board at a time

48 of the 70 marks. Input, then process, then output — each one finished and tested before the next is started.

Stage 7 — Input sub-system: construct, wire and test 48 marks · 7–9

Tasks 3a, 3b and 4b · Booklet p.14–18

Test the input board on its own before you wire anything to it. A fault found on one board takes minutes; the same fault found on three takes a lesson.

How marks get lost here
  • Switches labelled SW1, SW2, SW3 with no mention of what each one simulates.
  • Unused inputs left untied.
  • Components fitted diagonally.
  • Test points on the loom only, none at the chips.
  • Rows left undated — the dated tables are your record of testing.
  • The changes column left blank where nil should be written.
Construction — soldering, neatness and labelling → Testing — how to take each reading → Input board videos (below) →
Stage 8 — Process sub-system: construct, wire and test 48 marks · 7–9

Tasks 3a, 3b and 4b · Booklet p.14–18

Fit the sockets, test the board, and only then put the chips in. Check every tied pin against your layout before you power up.

Stage 9 — Output sub-system: construct, wire and test 48 marks · 7–9

Tasks 3a, 3b and 4b · Booklet p.14–18

When all three boards are joined, work through your truth table one row at a time. The row that behaves wrongly tells you which part of the circuit to look at.

Stage 10

Evaluation

Five marks that cost nothing but the habit of writing things down as you go.

Stage 10 — Evaluation 5 marks

Task 5 · Booklet p.19–20

The challenge does not have to be a disaster. A track cut in the wrong place, found and fixed, is a perfectly good challenge to write about.

How marks get lost here
  • A challenge named, but nothing said about what was done about it.
  • Logbook gaps, or entries that say only what was worked on.
