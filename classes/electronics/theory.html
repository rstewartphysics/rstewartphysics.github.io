Concept 1

Units, scientific notation & prefixes

Every quantity has an SI unit. Large and small values are written in scientific notation (e.g. 4 700 Ω = 4.7 × 10³ Ω) or with a prefix.

  • Charge Q — coulomb (C) · Current I — ampere (A)
  • Voltage / p.d. V — volt (V) · Resistance R — ohm (Ω)
  • Power P — watt (W) · Time t — second (s) · Capacitance C — farad (F)

Practice 1

Write each value in its base unit (no prefix): (a) 4.7 kΩ   (b) 22 µF   (c) 3.3 mA   (d) 470 nF.

Answer
(a) 4.7 kΩ = 4.7 × 1000 = 4700 Ω (b) 22 µF = 22 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.000022 F (c) 3.3 mA = 3.3 ÷ 1000 = 0.0033 A (d) 470 nF = 470 × 10⁻⁹ = 0.00000047 F

Practice 2

A 12 V supply drives a current of 0.25 A through a lamp. Calculate (a) the resistance of the lamp and (b) the power it dissipates.

Answer
(a) V = I R → R = V ÷ I = 12 ÷ 0.25 = 48 Ω (b) P = I V = 0.25 × 12 = 3.0 W

Concept 4

Voltage dividers & the comparator switch-over

Ratio form
V1V2 = R1R2
the p.d.s share in the ratio of the resistors
Output form
V2 = R2R1 + R2 × Vs
output across the lower resistor R₂
  • R₁ = top, R₂ = bottom. The output V2 is the p.d. across the bottom resistor.
  • Replace R₁ or R₂ with an LDR or thermistor to make a sensor whose output voltage changes with light or temperature.
  • Feed the divider output into a 741 comparator: when V2 rises above the reference voltage on the other input, the output switches over (low → high) and can drive a transistor/relay.

Practice 4

A divider across 5.0 V has R₁ = 4.0 kΩ (top) and an LDR as R₂ (bottom). In the dark the LDR is 16 kΩ. Calculate the output voltage in the dark.

Answer
V₂ = (R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)) × V_s V₂ = (16 / (4 + 16)) × 5.0 = (16/20) × 5.0 = 4.0 V
Concept 5

Analogue vs digital & the oscilloscope

Frequency
f = 1T
frequency = 1 ÷ period
  • An analogue signal varies smoothly through every value; a digital signal has only two levels (high/low, 1/0).
  • On an oscilloscope, peak voltage = (vertical divisions to the peak) × Y-gain (V/div).
  • Period T = (horizontal divisions for one wave) × time-base (s/div). Then f = 1 ÷ T.
Substitute & solve One complete wave spans 4.0 divisions. The time-base is set to 5.0 ms/division. Calculate the frequency.
Find the period
T = 4.0 × 5.0 = 20 ms
T = 0.020 s ms → s
f=1T
f=1 ÷ 0.020use seconds
f=50 Hz
Practice 5

A trace shows a peak 3.0 divisions high with the Y-gain at 2.0 V/division, and one wave spans 5.0 divisions with the time-base at 2.0 ms/division. State the peak voltage and calculate the frequency.

Answer
Peak voltage = 3.0 × 2.0 = 6.0 V T = 5.0 × 2.0 = 10 ms = 0.010 s f = 1 ÷ T = 1 ÷ 0.010 = 100 Hz
Concept 6

Capacitance & timing

  • A capacitor stores charge. It takes time to charge up through a resistor, so it introduces a delay.
  • A larger capacitance (or larger series resistance) lengthens the timing — the output takes longer to switch.
  • This is how a capacitor changes an output waveform's timing: it sets how long a timer (such as a 555 astable) stays high or low, which sets the frequency of the output.
Try it: the 555 Astable Simulator lets you change the capacitor and resistors and watch the on/off timing change.
Practice 6

In a timing circuit the capacitor is swapped from 10 µF to 100 µF, with the resistors unchanged. State and explain what happens to the time the output stays high.

Answer
The time increases (about ten times longer). A larger capacitance stores more charge, so it takes longer to charge up through the same resistor before the circuit switches state.
Concept 7

Magnetic effect & electromechanical devices

  • A current in a wire creates a magnetic field around it. The field is stronger when the wire is wound into a coil (and stronger still with an iron core).
  • This is used in electromechanical devices: the relay (a coil that switches a separate circuit), solenoid, motor and buzzer.
  • A relay lets a small electronic signal switch a much larger current, and keeps the two circuits separate.
Back-EMF: when a relay coil is switched off, its collapsing field produces a large reverse voltage that can destroy the transistor driving it. A flyback diode is fitted in reverse bias across the coil to safely conduct this away.
Practice 7

State why a flyback diode is connected across a relay coil, and explain why it must be fitted in reverse bias.

Answer
It protects the switching transistor from the large reverse voltage (back-EMF) produced when the coil's magnetic field collapses. In reverse bias it does not conduct during normal operation, but it conducts the back-EMF safely back round the coil when the current is switched off.
Concept 8

Combinational logic (overview)

  • Logic gates take digital inputs (1/0) and give a digital output set by a truth table.
  • Combinational logic connects gates together (≤4 inputs, ≤2 outputs) so the output depends only on the present inputs.
  • Useful building blocks: transistor switching, the bi-stable (a 1-bit memory), the half-adder (adds two bits) and the 741 comparator (switches when one input passes the other).

Practice 8

An alarm should sound only when a door is open AND the system is armed. State which single logic gate does this, and give the output when door = 1, armed = 0.

Answer
An AND gate. With inputs 1 and 0, an AND gate outputs 0 — the alarm stays off.
