What is an Engineer?

Engineers use science, maths, technology and creativity to solve real problems. In this unit you will learn what different engineers do, how engineered systems work, and how engineering can affect people, money and the environment.

The local case study for this unit is the flooding problem in Duns Park and the Bluidy Burn area.

What is engineering?

Engineering is about using science, maths, technology and creativity to solve real problems. Engineers design, build, test and improve things that people use every day.

Type of engineer Main work Examples
Electrical engineer Electricity, electronics, power, wiring and control systems. Lighting, sensors, motors, displays, power systems.
Mechanical engineer Moving parts, machines and forces. Engines, brakes, gears, wheels, pumps, turbines.
Civil engineer Buildings and infrastructure. Roads, bridges, tunnels, drainage, paths, flood defences.
Chemical engineer Materials and chemical processes. Fuels, plastics, coatings, water treatment, rust protection.

Engineers and systems

A system is a group of parts working together to do a job. Many systems have an input, a process, an output, and sometimes feedback.

System part Meaning Automatic door example
Input What goes into the system. A sensor detects a person.
Process What happens inside the system. The control system sends a signal.
Output The result. The doors open.
Feedback Information used to improve or control the system. The system checks the doors opened fully.

Impacts of engineering

Engineering can change people’s lives in good ways and bad ways. These changes are called impacts.

Social impact

Effects on people’s lives.

Examples: safety, comfort, travel, communication, access to services.

Economic impact

Effects on money and jobs.

Examples: costs, profits, jobs, trade, tourism, local businesses.

Environmental impact

Effects on the natural world.

Examples: habitats, pollution, carbon emissions, waste, energy use.

Answering with what + why

Weak answer: It makes it safer.

Better answer: One positive social effect is that people can use the path more safely. This matters because it reduces the risk of accidents and helps more people access the park.

Local case study: Duns Park and Bluidy Burn flood risk

Duns Park and the Bluidy Burn area give us a real local example of engineering. The challenge is not just to “stop water”. Engineers also have to think about access, safety, cost, habitats, land use and long-term climate resilience.

Known problem

  • Parts of Duns Park can become waterlogged and difficult to use.
  • The boardwalk and wetland area can be affected by flooding.
  • The issue is linked to saturated ground, rainfall and a rising water table.
  • The Bluidy Burn area is part of the wider flood-risk problem.

Engineering aim

  • Reduce flood risk.
  • Improve year-round access to the park.
  • Use nature-based solutions where possible.
  • Create or improve wetland and woodland habitats.
Which engineers could be involved?
Engineer What might they do? Why does it matter?
Civil engineer Design drainage routes, paths, culverts, ground works and flood-risk features. The park needs safe infrastructure that can cope with water and remain usable.
Environmental engineer Help design wetlands, habitats and nature-based flood management. The solution should reduce flooding without damaging the natural environment.
Electrical engineer Design sensors, monitoring systems or data-logging equipment for water levels. Data can help engineers check whether the solution is working over time.
Mechanical engineer Design or select pumps, gates or moving parts if these are needed. Moving systems must be reliable, safe and suitable for outdoor use.
Chemical/materials engineer Help choose durable materials, coatings and treatments for wet outdoor conditions. Materials must resist corrosion, weathering and long-term wear.

Duns Park as a system

Flood management can be thought of as a system. Water enters the area, the landscape and drainage features process or slow the water, and the output should be a safer, more usable park.

System part Duns Park example
Input Rainfall, surface water, groundwater and water from the Bluidy Burn area.
Process Wetlands, drainage routes, planting and land shaping slow, store or redirect water.
Output Reduced flood risk, improved access and better habitats.
Feedback Water-level data, site inspections and community feedback show whether the design is working.

Social, economic and environmental impacts of the Duns Park project

Social impacts

Positive: People may be able to use the park, paths and boardwalk more often.

Negative: Construction work may temporarily limit access or cause disruption.

Economic impacts

Positive: A more usable park may support visitors, local events and nearby businesses.

Negative: The project will cost money to design, build and maintain.

Environmental impacts

Positive: Wetland and woodland habitats may improve biodiversity.

Negative: Poorly planned work could disturb habitats during construction.
