Part 1

The speed of light is the same for everyone

Everyday speeds add and subtract. If you walk along a moving train, your speed relative to the platform is the train's speed plus or minus yours. Light does not follow this rule. Every observer measures the speed of light in a vacuum as c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1.

Two papers ask this (2016 and 2023): a source moves at 0.8c or 0.9c and emits light towards a stationary observer. State the speed the observer measures. Justify your answer.

Show the answer the marking instructions give

The speed of light is the same for all observers.
  • The statement is marked first. If it is wrong, both marks are lost.
  • A number with no unit scores 0.
  • Wrong physics in the justification loses that mark.

If every observer measures the same speed of light, something else has to change. Time and length are what change: the next two sections.

Part 2

Time dilation: the light clock

A light clock is a pulse of light bouncing between two mirrors. One tick is one trip up and back. When the clock moves past you, its light follows a longer, slanted path at the same speed c, so each tick takes longer. You measure the moving clock running slow. This is time dilation.

Part 3

Length contraction

A length along the direction of motion is measured shorter by an observer moving relative to it. This is length contraction. Check who is at rest relative to the object: they measure l. Sometimes the object moves past the observer; sometimes the observer moves past the object.

Part 3

Muons: two frames, one answer

Muons are made when cosmic rays enter the atmosphere, about 10 km above the surface of the Earth. They have a mean lifetime of 2.2 × 10−6 s in their frame of reference and travel at about 0.995c relative to an observer on Earth. Without relativity, very few would reach the ground. Far more are detected.

Exam skill: name the frame

Explain why a greater number of muons are detected on the surface of the Earth than would be expected if relativistic effects were not taken into account. (1 mark: 2019 specimen paper; the same question about pions in 2022.)

Show the answers the marking instructions accept
Either
The mean lifetime of the muon is greater for an observer in Earth's frame of reference.
Or
The mean distance travelled by a muon is shorter in the muon's frame of reference.
  • The answer must name, or clearly imply, a frame of reference. "Because of time dilation" on its own scores 0.
  • Do not mix the frames. In its own frame, the muon's mean lifetime is still 2.2 × 10−6 s.

Words to know

Frame of reference
The point of view an observer measures from, and moves with.
Relative speed
A speed measured by a stated observer. Always say what it is relative to.
Time dilation
A moving clock is measured to run slow: t′ is longer than t.
Length contraction
A length along the direction of motion is measured shorter by an observer moving relative to it: l′ is shorter than l.
Mean lifetime
The average time a particle such as a muon exists before it decays, measured in its own frame of reference.
Relativistic speed
A speed of about 0.1c or more, where the effects become significant.
