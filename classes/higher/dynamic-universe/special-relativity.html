Concept 1

Frames of reference and the speed of light

Every speed is measured relative to something: a frame of reference. At everyday speeds you add or subtract speeds to change frame. Light does not follow that rule. The speed of light in a vacuum is the same for all observers, however fast the observer or the source is moving.

Past papers 2023 P2 Q4(a), (b), (c)(i) 2018 S1 Q6 2016 S2 Q4(a), (b)(i) · asked in 3 of 10 exam years

On the data sheet
Speed of light in a vacuum
c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1
the same for all observers

Relative speed: add or subtract

  • A frame of reference is where a measurement is made from: the platform, the train, the spacecraft.
  • A passenger walking towards the front of a train: speed relative to the platform = train speed + walking speed.
  • Walking towards the back: train speed − walking speed. In the 2023 exam fewer than half of candidates got this one right.
  • Every speed you write needs its frame: "33.4 m s−1 relative to the platform".

Light breaks the rule

  • If light followed the train rule, light sent forwards from a spacecraft at 0.9c would reach the planet at 1.9c. It does not.
  • The observer on the spacecraft and the observer on the planet both measure the same speed: c = 3.00 × 108 m s−1.
  • Speed is distance ÷ time. If the speed of light cannot change, then time and length must be measured differently by observers moving relative to each other. That is Concepts 2 and 3.

Concept 2

Time dilation

A clock moving relative to an observer is measured to run slow. The observer measures a longer time t′ than the time t in the frame of reference of the moving clock. This is time dilation. It only becomes noticeable from about 0.1c upwards.

The light clock

  • A light clock bounces a pulse of light between two mirrors. One trip up and back is one tick.
  • In the spacecraft's frame the light goes straight up and down. The tick takes time t.
  • From Earth's frame the mirrors move sideways during the tick, so the light follows a longer path.
  • Light travels at c for both observers (Concept 1). A longer path at the same speed takes longer, so Earth measures a longer tick, t′.

Only near the speed of light

  • The graph shows how many times longer t′ is than t.
  • At 0.1c, t′ is only half a per cent longer. Trains, planes and rockets are far too slow for their clocks to fall noticeably behind.
  • At 0.6c it is 1.25 times longer; at 0.8c, 1.67 times; close to c it shoots up.
  • The evidence is fast particles. Particles made in accelerators and by cosmic rays have a longer mean lifetime in the lab than in their own frame.
  • Speed can be given as a fraction of c or in m s−1. Divide by 3.00 × 108 to get v/c.

Concept 3

Length contraction and muons

An object moving relative to an observer is measured to be shorter in its direction of motion. The observer measures a shorter length l′ than the length l measured by someone at rest relative to the object. This is length contraction. Muons reaching the ground are evidence for both effects.

On the relationship sheet
Length contraction
l′ = l √1 − (vc)2
multiply: l′ always comes out smaller than l
l′
length measured by the observer the object moves past (m)
l
length measured by someone at rest relative to the object (m)
v
speed of the object relative to the observer (m s−1)
c
speed of light, 3.00 × 108 m s−1

Shorter along the motion only

  • Only the length along the direction of motion shrinks. The height and width do not change.
  • A spacecraft: the crew measure l. An observer on a planet it passes measures the shorter l′.
  • A bridge, a tunnel or two detectors: someone standing beside them measures l. The fast spacecraft or particle moving past measures the shorter l′. The 2022, 2024 and 2025 papers all ask it this way round.
  • If your l′ comes out bigger than l, you divided instead of multiplying.

Muons: one result, two frames

  • Cosmic rays hitting the top of the atmosphere make muons. A muon's mean lifetime in its own frame is 2.2 µs. Even at nearly c, that is only about 660 m of travel, yet many muons reach the ground.
  • In Earth's frame (time dilation): the muons' mean lifetime is longer, t′ > t, so they travel further before they decay.
  • In the muons' frame (length contraction): the atmosphere moves past them, so its depth is shorter, l′ < l, and the ground reaches them before they decay.
  • An exam answer must name its frame: "In the frame of reference of the muon, the distance to the ground is shorter." With no frame, it scores 0.
